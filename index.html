Introduction

Every book tells a story. Together, books tell us something about the world we read in.

Books offer a window into changing cultural interests, reading preferences, and the global publishing industry. By examining book-related data, we can explore which genres appear across publication years, how ratings are distributed among genres and publishers, and where bestselling authors come from.

This dashboard brings together three visualisations to investigate these questions. Each offers a different perspective on books and reading, helping us discover patterns, compare distributions, and explore the diversity of the literary world.

Popularity Is More Than Numbers

A book’s popularity can tell a story, but more books do not always mean better books.

Titles with more ratings and reviews attract greater reader attention, suggesting that some books become shared reading experiences while others remain less widely explored.

This uneven distribution shows how readers’ attention concentrates on a smaller number of popular titles.

Beyond the Crowd

Look beyond the most popular titles, and there may be hidden gems waiting to be discovered. Books with fewer ratings or reviews are not necessarily less enjoyable; they may simply have reached a smaller audience.

Popularity measures how much attention a book receives, not how much a reader will love it. Readers may explore popular titles out of curiosity, trends or recommendations, but their ratings reflect their actual reading experience.

A Longer Journey, Slightly Higher Ratings

The trend line tells a gentle story:
Books with more pages tend to receive slightly higher average ratings.

Perhaps readers enjoy getting lost in richly imagined worlds and spending more time with the characters they grow to love. Yet, every reader is different, and even the longest stories do not always earn the highest praise.

Where Most Reading Stories Begin

Most books in the dataset are shorter, while lengthy novels appear less frequently.

With so many shorter books shaping the pattern, the relationship between page count and ratings becomes less certain for longer titles. After all, some stories captivate us in a few pages, while others take hundreds to leave their mark.

Romance Rules the Shelves

Love stories take centre stage. Romance leads the way in book counts, with Fiction also holding a strong presence. From sweeping love stories to imaginative worlds, these genres capture a substantial share of the books publishers bring to readers.

Authors Who Refuse to Stay in One Lane

Why tell just one kind of story? Many authors venture across multiple genres, exploring different themes, styles, and audiences. Their publishing journeys show that creativity doesn't always fit neatly into a single category.

Beyond the Bestsellers

Poetry, science, biography, short stories, and dark academia appear less frequently than the leading genres. Yet fewer titles don't mean less value. These quieter corners of the literary world offer distinctive perspectives, niche interests, and stories waiting to be discovered.

Different Publishers, Different Priorities

Some publishers spread their books across a wide range of genres, while others concentrate on particular categories. These contrasting patterns reveal different publishing identities — from broad literary portfolios to more specialised areas of focus.

The Generational Reading Climb

Reading participation climbs with each generation, rising from just 11.2% among Generation Z to 47.4% in the Interwar generation. The biggest leap comes between Generation X and Baby Boomers, where participation jumps from 17.6% to 30.2%.

Women Take the Lead

Women lead the reading race across every generation. The gap is especially striking in Generation X, where female participation (23.4%) is almost double that of males (11.7%). Yet among the oldest generation, the race tightens, with only a 3.3 percentage-point difference between females and males.

Generation Z Takes the Lead

Generation Z leads the reading race, averaging 2.1 hours per day among those who read. However, this lead is largely driven by males, who spent 2.8 hours reading compared with 1.6 hours for females. In contrast, reading time across the other generations remained relatively steady, ranging from 1.3 hours for Millennials and Generation X to 1.6 hours for the Interwar generation.

A Narrowing Gender Gap

As generations grow older, the gender gap in reading time narrows. While Generation Z shows a 1.2-hour difference between males and females, the gap falls to just 0.1 hours among Baby Boomers.
